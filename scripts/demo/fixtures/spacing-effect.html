Why forgetting helps you remember

The spacing effect, and what a century of research says about how to study.

By the Margins desk · 6 min read

In 1885 Hermann Ebbinghaus memorised lists of nonsense syllables and tested himself at intervals. Most of what he learned was gone within a day, yet each review brought it back faster than the last, and it then stayed longer.

Forgetting is not the enemy of memory; it is the signal that tells the brain a memory is worth keeping. When a fact has started to fade, retrieving it takes effort, and that effort is what strengthens the trace.

Cramming feels productive because the material is fresh. Spreading the same hours over several days feels harder, and it is: you keep meeting the material just as it slips away. That friction is the point.

The practical rule is simple. Review new material after a day, then after three days, then after a week. Each gap should be long enough that recall takes a moment of real work.
